Two ways into the same moment.

Mara arrives at her mother's house and finds her brother there. She is glad he came. She wishes she did not need him.

What those numbers leave open

Shorter sentences may feel brisk, hesitant, emphatic—or something else in context. More quoted words do not automatically mean better dialogue. Read the passages before deciding what a difference means.

Read sample A · sample-a.md

Mara found the spare key in the blue cup. She picked it up. Put it down. Picked it up again.

“You're early,” her brother said.

“The bus was early.”

“It never is.”

“Well. Today.”

He stood in the doorway with a dish towel over his shoulder. Their mother's dish towel. The one with the cherries. Mara wanted to take it from him. She wanted him to keep drying the dishes so she would not have to.

“I made tea,” he said.

“I don't want tea.”

“All right.”

He moved aside. She stepped in. Nothing had changed, except the shoes were gone from under the radiator. That was enough. She stared at the clean strip of floor.

“I might have half a cup.”

He nodded once, as if half a cup were a thing he knew how to manage.

Mara left the key in her coat pocket. She was not ready to put it back.

Read sample B · sample-b.md

The spare key was still in the blue cup beside the gate, where their mother had insisted it was safe because nobody looking for a key would think to look in something so obvious. Mara closed her hand around it and stood listening to the faint clatter of dishes from inside, relieved that her brother had arrived first and irritated with him for making her feel relieved.

When he opened the door, he had the cherry-patterned dish towel over his shoulder. She almost reached for it. For years he had been the one who needed feeding, fetching, reminding, and now he was standing in their mother's doorway as though there were practical things left to do and he could do them.

He said there was tea. Mara said she did not want any, then noticed the bare strip beneath the radiator where their mother's shoes had always waited, toes turned toward the door.

“Half a cup, perhaps.”

He stepped aside without asking her to explain. She kept the key in her pocket as she passed him; returning it to the cup would have meant deciding something about whether she belonged here, and for the moment she wanted only to sit down.

One question is enough.

Which passage—or which part of either passage—feels closer to what you want today?

“A bit of both,” “neither,” and “I’m not sure” are useful answers. You can keep a difference on purpose.

How to read this fairly
  • These are descriptions of the supplied passages, not a stable author profile or a quality score.
  • Short samples change easily. The demonstration is intentionally short so you can see what changes.
  • Sentence boundaries and quotation handling follow the toolkit's English-oriented rules. Unusual dialogue formatting can affect results.
  • Quoted words are an estimate of dialogue: a quoted letter or thought can be counted too.
  • Subject, scene purpose and editing stage can explain a difference. A difference need not be repaired.
